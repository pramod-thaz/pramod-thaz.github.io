Scenario Details

Let’s assume an investor goes long (buys) 1 S&P 500 E-mini Futures Contract.

ParameterValueNotes
Initial Futures Price5,000 index pointsTotal Contract Value = $5,000 \times $50 = $250,000$
Contract Multiplier$50 per pointEvery 1-point price move = $50 gain/loss
Initial Margin Required$12,000Upfront cash deposited
Maintenance Margin$10,000Minimum required account balance
Margin Call Buffer$2,000$(\text{Initial Margin} - \text{Maintenance Margin})$

1. How Much Can the Price Fall Before Triggering a Margin Call?

A margin call occurs when your cumulative losses exceed the buffer between your initial margin ($12,000) and your maintenance margin ($10,000).

$$\text{Maximum Allowed Loss} = \text{Initial Margin} - \text{Maintenance Margin} = $12,000 - $10,000 = \mathbf{$2,000}$$ Now, convert that dollar loss into index points:

$$\text{Max Allowed Drop (in Points)} = \frac{\text{Maximum Allowed Loss}}{\text{Contract Multiplier}} = \frac{$2,000}{$50/\text{point}} = \mathbf{40\text{ points}}$$

Break-Even & Margin Call Thresholds:

$$\text{Margin Call Trigger Price} = 5,000 - 40 = \mathbf{4,960\text{ points}}$$

$$\text{Percentage Drop Required} = \frac{40}{5,000} = \mathbf{0.80%}$$

Takeaway: The futures price only needs to fall by 0.80% (40 points) for the investor to get a margin call.

A. FACTOR BASED STRATEGIES

Index strategies use ==rules-based replication== of an index (e.g., S&P 500) → ↑transparency, ↓costs, diversification, tax efficiency (low turnover).

CAPM limitation: Only market beta explains returns. Evidence supports additional systematic risk factors (and associated premiums): “SSM-QDV”

  • Size: Small-cap premium (compensation for higher risk)
  • Style (Value): Low P/E & P/B outperform growth (high P/E, high EPS growth)
  • Momentum: Prior winners continue to outperform (short-term)
  • Quality: Consistent earnings + low debt → outperform in downturns
  • Dividend Yield: High-yield stocks outperform in declining rate environments
  • Volatility (Low-Vol): Lower-vol stocks outperform in high-uncertainty periods

Passive Factor-Based (PFB) Strategy:

  • Factor premiums treated as given (passive).
  • Only active decision = ==desired factor betas/exposures.==
  • Simple model: $r=βfactor×Premiumfactor r = \beta_{\text{factor}} \times \text{Premium}_{\text{factor}} r=βfactor​×Premiumfactor​$

Factor-based index (valid benchmark) requires transparent rules on: factor selection, betas/exposures, rebalancing.

vs Cap-Weighted Index:

  • Factor index weights based on fundamentals (size, value, earnings, yield, etc.) → ==less prone to mispricing (overvalued stocks not overweighted)==. ==Cap-weighted indexes are prone to mispricing== because overvalued stocks are over-weighted based on price alone.
  • Cap-weighted dominated by largest stocks; ==any outperformance of factor index often = extra pay for extra risk (not alpha).==
  • Result: ==High tracking error (TE)== vs cap-weighted.
  • Fees: higher than cap-weighted but lower than pure active. #EXAMPLE

B. APPROACHES TO PASSIVE EQUITY INVESTING

Three main approaches:

1. Pooled Funds

  • Mutual Funds (MFs):
    • Easy unit purchase, record-keeping by sponsor, many options available.
    • Manager handles all ops: rebalancing, adds/deletes, reinvest cash flows, proxy voting.
    • CONS: Tax-inefficient (must sell stocks on redemptions → capital gains); cannot trade intra-day; higher fees.
  • ETFs:
    • Trade like stocks (margin if bullish, short if bearish).
    • ==In-kind creation/redemption → highly tax-efficient (no cap gains triggered on ETF share sales).==
    • Wider variety of indexes (easier factor exposure, e.g., long small-cap ETF + short large-cap ETF).
    • ==Lower management fees than MFs==.
    • Tradeoff: ==ETF (lower fee) vs MF (lower trading costs — no commissions/spreads).==
    • CONS: Trading costs (bid-ask spreads, commissions); potential large discount to NAV if underlying basket illiquid (e.g., EM ETFs).

2. Derivative-Based Strategies

  • Synthetic exposure: T-bill + long index futures = index return.
  • Advantages: ==Very low cost, leverage (margin only), high liquidity for tactical shifts/rebalancing/equitizing cash, efficient overlays.==
  • Disadvantages: #imp
    • OTC (forwards/swaps): counterparty risk + illiquidity.
    • ==Long options: lose full premium if OTM.==
    • Daily ==settlement== → cash flow management + margin calls.
    • Must ==roll contract==s for long-term exposure (best used as overlay, not core holding).
    • ==Basis risk== (imperfect correlation with index). #EXAMPLE
    • Regulations may restrict availability; mostly institutional only. #EXAMPLE

#EXAMPLE

The three requirements for an index to be the basis for an equity investment strategy are: (a) rule-based, (b) transparent, and (c) investable. Buffering (Statement 2) makes benchmarks more investable by gradual/ orderly index transitions.
Free-float adjustment increases (does not lower) investability/liquidity because it excludes non-tradable shares (founders/government/etc.), accurately reflecting true liquidity.

Four approaches:

  1. Full Replication ==Hold every index constituent at exact index weights==. Obstacles: Thousands of stocks, ==large AUM== required, ==illiquid== names → ==↑TE==, constant data feed needed from index provider. #EXAMPLE
  2. Stratified Sampling Partition index into mutually exclusive, exhaustive, proportional strata (by industry, size, style, domicile, etc.). Hold a subset of stocks in each stratum to match factor exposures. ✓ Works with ==smaller portfolios and illiquid/large indexes.== #EXAMPLE ==Stratified sampling is used for indexes with large # constituents or low AUM; it holds fewer securities than the index while still tracking closely and reducing brokerage costs==
  3. Optimization Mathematical program: minimize TE subject to constraints (e.g., max 100 most liquid stocks). Relies on historical correlations. ==Use historical $\rho$ (correlations) to minimize $TE$ with fewer stocks. match index characteristics/performance== Risk: ==Correlations break down → frequent re-optimization → high turnover & trading costs.==
  4. Blended Approach Full replication on liquid/large-cap portion (e.g., top 1,000); stratified sampling or optimization on remainder (e.g., next 2,000). ==Index split into sub-indexes, each tracked optimally.==

D. MANAGING TRACKING ERROR

  • Numerical Step-Logic (Worked Example):
    • Step 1: $AR_t = R_{PF} - R_{Index}$.
    • Step 2: $\overline{AR} = \frac{\sum AR_t}{n}$.
    • Step 3: $TE = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(AR_t - \overline{AR})^2}{n-1}}$.
  • Numerical Mapping (Fund ABC): Mean $AR = 2%$, $TE = 0.7%$.
    • Math: $2% \pm (1 \times 0.7%)$.
    • Result: 68% probability active returns fall between 1.3% and 2.7%.

Goal of index manager: ==Non-negative active return + low TE.==

Factors that increase TE:

  • Larger number of index constituents (↑ trading costs).
  • Higher management fees.
  • Sampling or optimization (vs full replication).
  • Cash drag (portfolio not fully invested while index is); mitigate by equitizing cash with index futures.
  • Intra-day trading (index uses closing prices); mitigate by trading near market close.

TE control: minimize trading costs, net investor flows, equitization via derivatives to offset cash drag

  • Style exposures (size, value/growth, momentum, quality, etc.)

Ways to enhance passive returns:

  • Securities Lending: Earn lender fee on shares lent out. Risks: Counterparty fails to return shares, collateral value drops (market risk), lose voting rights during loan (but borrower must compensate dividends). #EXAMPLE
  • Shareholder Activism: Pressure portfolio companies (sell unprofitable divisions, cut costs). Index funds/pensions have voting power. Trade-off: Absolute returns may rise, but higher costs → portfolio likely lags index net of costs.
