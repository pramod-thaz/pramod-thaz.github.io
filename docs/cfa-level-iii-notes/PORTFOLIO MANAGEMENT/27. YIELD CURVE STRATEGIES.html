CFA Level III Notes

YC strategies ==use Treasury Bonds (pure interest rate play).== This avoids influence from credit and liquidity risk. ==Par yield curve: stylized representation of YTM at various maturities; often extracted from available bond yields via a model (not directly traded Framework: $$ \text{YTM (Corp)} = \text{YTM (Treasury)} + \text{Spread} $$

  • Corp Bonds: exposed to Interest rate + Credit + Liquidity risk
  • Treasury Bonds: exposed only to interest rate risk

Yield curve strategies structure a portfolio so that it profits from changes in the yield curve (Δyc).

A. Types of Yield Curve Strategies

There are three primary sources of yield curve change:

1. Change in Level (Parallel Shifts)

  • Parallel Up: Entire yield curve shifts upward by the same amount.
  • Parallel Down: Entire yield curve shifts downward by the same amount.

2. Change in Slope

Slope of the yield curve is typically measured by the 30-year minus 2-year spread: $$ \text{Slope} = \text{YTM}{30} - \text{YTM}{2} $$

  • Steepening: Spread increases (long rates rise relative to short rates, or short rates fall relative to long rates).
  • Flattening: Spread decreases.
  • Inverted: ==When $\text{YTM}{30} < \text{YTM}{2}$ (negative spread).==

3. Change in Curvature (Butterfly)

Curvature is measured using the Butterfly Spread (body vs. wings): $$ \text{Butterfly Spread} = 2 \times \text{YTM}{\text{Middle (Body)}} - (\text{YTM}{\text{Short (Wing)}} + \text{YTM}_{\text{Long (Wing)}}) $$

  • When the body rises above the wings (middle yields increase relative to short and long), ==the curve becomes more curved (positive butterfly).==
  • The three sources of ΔYC (Level, Slope, Curvature) can occur simultaneously and are uncorrelated.

B. Factors Affecting Fixed Income Portfolio Return

Recall the Taylor expansion for bond price change:

$$ % \Delta P \approx (-D \times \Delta r) + \frac{1}{2} C \times (\Delta r)^2 $$

  • Duration (D): Linear (first-order) approximation of %ΔP — larger effect.
  • Convexity (C): Curvature (second-order) term — smaller effect but important for large moves or non-parallel shifts.

C. The Role of Convexity

Convexity ($C$) captures the curvature in the price-yield relationship. It measures the gap between the actual price and the linear (duration-only) estimate.

Advantages of Higher Convexity #EXAMPLE

  • Price rises faster when yields fall.
  • Price falls slower when yields rise.
  • Acts as ==insurance== against yield volatility. The improved price performance outweighs the higher initial price paid.

Disadvantages of Higher Convexity

  • Holding duration constant, ==higher-convexity bonds are more expensive (higher price → lower yield).==
  • If yields do not move much, ==you overpaid for convexity you didn’t need.==

D. Carry Trade: Inter-Market

Goal: Borrow in a low-yield currency/market and invest in a high-yield currency/market. Expected profit = yield spread (leveraged strategy).

Example:

  • UK yields: 6%
  • Japan yields: 4%
  • FX: ¥150 / £ If no change in yields or FX:
  • Borrow in Japan at 4% → Invest in UK at 6% → 2% spread profit. Balance Sheet View:

Risks of Inter-Market Carry Trade

  1. Interest rate risk: UK yields rise → UK bond prices fall → Assets ↓
  2. FX risk:
    • £ depreciates → Assets (UK bonds in £ terms) ↓
    • Yen appreciates → Liabilities (Japan borrowing) ↑ in £ terms
  3. Duration mismatch between assets and liabilities → increases interest rate exposure.
  4. Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (UIRP) does not hold in practice. Yield differentials are not always offset by expected FX changes.

Alternative ==via Currency Swap:== Investor effectively:

  • Holds UK bond (earns £ fixed)
  • Issues Japan debt (pays ¥ floating/fixed) ==Exposes investor to same risk factors as spot carry trade.==

E. Carry Trade: Intra-Market

Borrow and invest within the same market/currency (typically along the yield curve).

Example:

  • Short 4-year bond @ 3% (liability)
  • Long 10-year bond @ 5% (asset)
  • Net spread/profit = 2%

Assumes no change in the yield curve. Risk = yield curve changes (especially steepening or level rise).

Alternatives to Spot Carry Trade

1. ==Interest Rate Swap==

  • Investor receives fixed (long-term ==average yield==) and pays floating (LIBOR).
  • Equivalent to: Long a long-term bond + Short (issue) short-term floating debt.
  • ==Profit declines if rates rise: (Fixed received - Floating paid) decreases.==

  • Spot: Short ST bonds → Long LT bonds
  • Swap: Receive fixed → Pay floating
  • Futures: Long bond futures

F. Strategies if Expect No Change in Yields

Scenario: Upward-sloping yield curve (LT > ST) + Expect yields to remain stable.

I. Buy & Hold Long-Maturity (Long Duration) Bonds

  • Over a multi-year horizon, the higher yield of the long bond compounds to higher total return than rolling short-term bonds (which stay at low ST rates).

==More aggressive (levered) version — “Repo Carry”:==

  • Short (borrow) 1-year bond @ 3%
  • Long (invest) 10-year bond @ 5%
  • Earn 2% spread with no net capital (levered carry trade).

#EXAMPLE The fall in long-term yields-to-maturity will lead to price appreciation under the “buy-and-hold” strategy. III. Sell Convexity

  • Higher-convexity bonds are expensive (lower yield).
  • If you expect low volatility (no large Δr), sell high-Cx bonds and buy low-Cx bonds to capture the convexity premium (higher yield).

Ways to sell convexity:

G. Strategies if Expect a Change in Yields

  • ==Expect yields to fall → Lengthen portfolio duration (maximize price gains).==

The Math: Step-by-Step Scenarios
To make the math simple, we assume a 10-Year Bond has a DV01 of $70 per million, and a 2-Year Bond has a DV01 of $20 per million.
  1. The Duration-Neutral Steepener Example
  • Goal: Insulate the trade from parallel rate shifts. Profit only if the curve steepens.
  • The Sizing Math: You must match total dollar durations.
    • Short $1,000,000 of 10Y = -$70 DV01
    • Buy $3,500,000 of 2Y = +$70 DV01 ($3.5M × $20)
    • Net Portfolio DV01 = $0
  • The Profit Mechanics:
    • If Bear Steepening: Overall rates go up. 10Y yield spikes by 10 bps; 2Y yield rises by only 2 bps.
      • 10Y Short makes: 10 bps × $70 = +$700
      • 2Y Long loses: 2 bps × -$70 = -$140
      • Net Profit: +$560
    • If Bull Steepening: Overall rates go down. 2Y yield plunges by 10 bps; 10Y yield drops by only 2 bps.
      • 2Y Long makes: 10 bps × $70 = +$700
      • 10Y Short loses: 2 bps × -$70 = -$140
      • Net Profit: +$560
  1. The Duration-Neutral Flattener Example
  • Goal: Insulate the trade from parallel rate shifts. Profit only if the curve flattens.
  • The Sizing Math: You swap the legs but keep the exact same DV01 weighting.
    • Buy $1,000,000 of 10Y = +$70 DV01
    • Short $3,500,000 of 2Y = -$70 DV01
    • Net Portfolio DV01 = $0
  • The Profit Mechanics:
    • If Bear Flattening: Overall rates go up. 2Y yield spikes by 10 bps; 10Y yield rises by only 2 bps.
      • 2Y Short makes: 10 bps × $70 = +$700
      • 10Y Long loses: 2 bps × -$70 = -$140
      • Net Profit: +$560
    • If Bull Flattening: Overall rates go down. 10Y yield plunges by 10 bps; 2Y yield drops by only 2 bps.
      • 10Y Long makes: 10 bps × $70 = +$700
      • 2Y Short loses: 2 bps × -$70 = -$140
      • Net Profit: +$560

J. Strategies for Curvature Changes in Yield Curve

I. Expect Yield Curve to Become More Curved (-ve Butterfly)

![](_rect_imgs/27. X Yield-Curve-Strategies -merged__27_Yield_Curve_Strategies_1_p19_63-564.png)

  • Long Butterfly (also called “long wings, short body”):
    • Long Barbell (short + long maturities — the “wings”)
    • Short Bullet (intermediate maturity — the “body”) ![](Pasted image 20260701191441.png) Parameters: Levered Strategy
  • ==Overall portfolio duration = 0 (market-neutral to parallel shifts).==
  • ==Net long convexity (barbell has higher Cx than bullet).==

Benefits:

  • Profits if curvature increases (body underperforms wings).
  • ==Also profits if curve flattens.==
  • Benefits from ==rising volatility (higher Cx).==

Risks: Costs more (lower yield); underperforms if curvature does not increase or volatility falls.

II. Expect Yield Curve to ==Become Less Curved== (+ve Butterfly)

  • Short Butterfly:

    • Long Bullet (body)
    • Short Barbell (wings)

  • Net ==short convexity.==

  • Profits if ==curvature decreases or volatility falls.==

  • ==Also benefis if YC steepens==

III. Condor (Variation of Butterfly)

![](_rect_imgs/27. X Yield-Curve-Strategies -merged__27_Yield_Curve_Strategies_1_p21_63-395.png)

  • Spreads the “body” across two intermediate points (e.g., 5yr + 20yr) instead of concentrating on one (e.g., 10yr).
  • More ==conservative than butterfly: smaller profit/loss if curvature changes, because long and short cash flows are better matched.==
  • ==Lower dispersion → lower net convexity vs. pure butterfly.==

K. Yield Curve Strategies Across Currencies

International investing offers diversification and higher return potential, but introduces FX risk.

Example: UK investor buys 2-year zero-coupon US Treasury (currently trading at 95, par $100m, net value $95m).

  • Spot FX: $1.20 / £ (or £0.8333 / $)
  • Cost in £ today: £79.167m

After 1 year (assume):

  • US Treasury value: $97.2m
  • Expected FX: $1.14 / £ (£0.8772 / $)
  • Proceeds in £: £85.263m
  • Total Return in £: +7.7% ![](_rect_imgs/27. X Yield-Curve-Strategies -merged__27_Yield_Curve_Strategies_1_p22_70-544.png) Return Breakdown:
  • US Treasury local return: +2.315%
  • FX return (£ appreciation): +5.268%
  • Approximate: $R_{DC} \approx R_{FC} + R_{FX} = 7.583%$
  • Exact: $(1 + R_{DC}) = (1 + R_{FC})(1 + R_{FX})$ → $R_{DC} = 7.7%$

Risks (Unhedged):

  • ==US Treasury price risk (sold before maturity).==
  • ==FX risk on principal and any coupons.==

Hedging:

  • ==Sell FX forward==
  • Use currency swap (==synthetic foreign bond exposure==)

Hedging with Currency Swap:

  • Creates synthetic exposure.
  • In practice, a ==basis spread exists between direct and synthetic (swap) foreign yields.==
  • If basis is positive, synthetic may be cheaper or more expensive depending on sign. ![](_rect_imgs/27. X Yield-Curve-Strategies -merged__27_Yield_Curve_Strategies_1_p23_68-467.png)

L. Evaluating Expected Return of Yield Curve Strategies

Example: 3-year, 5% coupon bond trading at par (YTM = 5%). 1-year investment horizon.

Expected yield changes over next year:

  • Roll-down (if curve shape unchanged): Δr (roll) = -10 bps (YTM falls as bond shortens).
  • Constant-maturity change: Δr (CM) = +30 bps (yields at the remaining maturity expected to rise).

Total expected yield change: $$ E(\Delta r) = -0.10% + 0.30% = +0.20% $$

==Framework== for Expected Return

$$ E(R) = \text{Income} + % \Delta P_{\text{roll}} + % \Delta P_{\text{CM}} $$

Step-by-step calculation (detailed in notes):

  • Income (coupon): +5%
  • Roll-down price change: +0.186%
  • Constant-maturity price change (using duration ≈ 1.9 at t=2): -0.57%
  • Total E(R) ≈ 4.616% ![](_rect_imgs/27. X Yield-Curve-Strategies -merged__27_Yield_Curve_Strategies_1_p24_69-51.png) Alternative (total ΔP approach) yields similar result (~4.63%).
