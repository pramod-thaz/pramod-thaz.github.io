CFA Level III Notes

A. Managing Liquidity Risk

Financial institutions must maintain sufficient liquidity to meet obligations at all times.

  • Banks: Depositor withdrawals
  • Insurance companies: Policy claims (death, accidents, etc.)

Endowment Fund Example (ABC Endowment supporting ABC University)

Cash Inflows (c/i): Donations + Investment returns
Cash Outflows (c/o):

  • Distributions to university (spending requirements)
  • Capital calls on PE fund commitments

During Crisis — Liquidity Crunch Dynamics

  • ==Contributions ↓ (donors face hardships)==
  • ==Capital calls ↑ (PE managers buy distressed assets)==
  • ==Investment returns ↓ (capital losses mount)==
  • ==Spending ↑ (university revenues fall → needs more support)==

Result: c/i ↓↓ and c/o ↑ → Liquidity Crunch. Endowment may need to cut distributions or borrow.

Liquidity Budget (Key Planning Tool)

Categorize assets by time required to convert to cash without significant market impact.

Weighted liquidity available =

Tradeoff: More liquid assets → lower expected yield. ==Illiquid assets → higher yield (illiquidity premium) but greater liquidity risk.==

B. Managing Illiquid Assets

Illiquid assets (PE, real estate, certain hedge funds) are difficult and costly to rebalance.

1. Calendar-Based Rebalancing

Rebalance at fixed intervals (e.g., quarter-end). ==Reduces transaction costs but allows drift from SAA between dates.==

2. Percentage-Based (Band) Rebalancing

Trigger rebalance when allocation breaches a pre-set band (e.g., small cap target 10–15%). Rebalance to outer bound or midpoint.

Key insight: Narrow bands → frequent rebalancing & high costs. ==Use wider bands for more volatile/illiquid asset classes.==

3. Automatic Adjustment Mechanisms

Instead of trading the illiquid asset directly, adjust a correlated liquid proxy.

Example: If PE exposure exceeds its limit due to appreciation, reduce public equity allocation to keep overall equity risk in check.
Caution: This increases overall portfolio illiquidity (liquidity risk rises).

PE-Specific Note: Capital calls occur over time; distributions come at the end. Investors have limited control during ramp-up and wind-down phases. ==Diversify PE holdings across vintage years to avoid concentration/maturity risk.==

4. Stress Testing

Simulate crisis scenarios (donations ↓, capital calls ↑, returns ↓, spending ↑) to verify the endowment can meet long-term objectives without forced sales at depressed prices.

5. Using Derivatives for Liquidity

Example: Want +$50M bond exposure.

  • Buying bonds directly uses full $50M capital.
  • ==Long bond futures (margin ≈ $5M) → instant exposure + $45M capital freed== → higher portfolio liquidity.

#EXAMPLE

  • Rebalancing band principle: Wider bands for higher-volatility assets; narrower bands for lower-volatility assets (compare σ to range)
  • Cash: Lowest σ but widest bands → reduce band width
  • Private equity: Highest σ but tightest bands → expand band width

C. Illiquidity Premium

Core Principle: ==The more illiquid the asset and/or the longer the expected illiquidity period, the higher the illiquidity premium (additional return) investors require.==

==Approaches== to Estimating Illiquidity Premium

1. Put Option (Liquidity) Value Approach

A put gives the right to sell → effectively generates liquidity (cash flow).

For equivalent assets differing only in liquidity:

$$ P(\text{illiquid asset}) + \text{Value of Put (liquidity)} = P(\text{liquid asset}) $$

$$ \text{Illiquidity Premium ($)} = P(\text{liquid}) - P(\text{illiquid}) $$ #EXAMPLE

  • Illiquidity premium = expected compensation for locking up capital for an uncertain period
  • Estimation via put-option model: discount size = value of a put option struck at the hypothetical “marketable price” of the illiquid asset at purchase
  • formula: illiquid asset price = marketable price − put price
  • If both prices are known/estimated, compute expected return for each; the spread = illiquidity premium (%)

#EXAMPLE

(Bonds identical in interest rate & credit risk, differ only in liquidity):

BondLiquidityPriceYield
AHigh1006%
BLow1038%

Illiquidity Premium = ==2% (yield) or $3 (price differential).== (due to liquidity, credit risk, co size)

2. Index-Based Approach

$$ \text{Illiquidity Premium (%)} \approx R(\text{Index of Illiquid Assets}) - R(\text{Index of Liquid Assets}) $$

Limitations:

  • I==ndex is a broad average== → may under- or over-state the premium for specific assets.
  • Observed spread ==may include other premia (credit risk, size, etc.).==
  • Requires ==attribution analysis to isolate the pure liquidity component==.

#EXAMPLE

  • Endowment should target longer-term (more illiquid) investments within asset classes to capture highest liquidity premium
  • Literature confirms positive illiquidity–return relationship in public equity, but premium is hard to isolate from market, value, and size factors
  • Illiquidity premium estimates use broad indexes; actual investors hold small subsets, so experience varies widely and is exposed to idiosyncratic risk
  • These challenges underscore need for liquidity budgeting to capture illiquidity premium while controlling risk

D. Asset Allocation in Relation to Liquidity Needs

Crisis Impact on Private Equity Liquidity

  • ==Capital calls accelerate: PE managers find distressed opportunities → investors must deploy capital faster than planned.==
  • ==Distributions decline: Managers postpone exits to avoid selling at distressed prices.==
  • ==Valuation smoothing/lag: PE appraised values (based on historical comps) lag public markets. In a crisis, public prices fall but PE values may appear overstated → reported exposure looks inflated while true liquidity is worse.==

Recommendation: Apply unsmoothing techniques to obtain realistic volatility and correlation estimates.

#EXAMPLE #explain

The design of the spending rate policy incorporates a ==smoothing, counter-cyclical element,== leading to ==spending rates below 5% in a period of sustained strong investment returns but higher than 5% in a protracted weak return environment.== This design of the spending rate policy ==exacerbates the endowment’s liquidity needs in severe market downturns.==

Illustrative Liquidity Pressure Example

Endowment distributes fixed $5M/year.

  • Normal: $100M PF → liquidity need = 5%
  • Crisis: PF drops to $70M → liquidity ==need rises to ≈7.1% ($5M / $70M)==

Problem: Liquidity requirement increases precisely when assets are becoming more illiquid.

==Tools== for Managing Liquidity Risk (Institutional)

  1. Cash Flow Forecasting - ==forecasting models and commitment-pacing models==

    • Outflows: Forecast spending + capital call drawdowns. Diversify PE across vintage years.
    • Inflows: Donations (highly procyclical — fall sharply in crises).

  2. Liquidity Budget — Map % of portfolio by conversion horizon (see 30.1).

  3. Stress Testing — Ensure ability to meet commitments without liquidating high-risk assets at fire-sale prices.

#EXAMPLE

Project endowment distributions to the university as cash outflows, governed by a ~5% annual spending rate policy that aims to preserve purchasing power.

  • In stress periods, distributions from prior private investments may cease (GPs struggle to exit), while future capital commitments remain legal obligations; GPs may accelerate calls in depressed markets.
  • Diversify across fund vintage years to avoid cycle concentration; commit capital steadily to limit large allocation shifts and transaction costs.
  • Avoid outsized allocations to few funds to reduce idiosyncratic risk.
  • Donor inflows typically drop sharply in stress, raising liquidity needs; liquidating risk/high‑beta assets after losses is valuation‑unfriendly.
  • Access to top private‑market managers is competitive and may be unavailable when the portfolio seeks to increase allocations.
  • Develop a liquidity budget setting minimum liquidity targets based on conversion timeframes; monitor it regularly.
  • Run regular stress tests (historical & hypothetical scenarios) to gauge liquidity‑profile drift and verify the minimum budget is met.

E. Asset Allocation in Relation to Risk/Return (Endowments)

Typical Endowment Objectives

  1. Meet current spending requirements of beneficiary → Real return component (e.g., 5%)
  2. Maintain long-term purchasing power → Inflation component (e.g., 2%)

$$ \text{Required Nominal Return} \approx r_{\text{real spending}} + \pi $$

Spending Policy (Smoothed)

Annual spending is usually a weighted average of:

  • Last year’s actual distribution (S_{t-1})
  • Long-term policy rate = (Long-term real geometric average return × Beginning VPF) <–==note beginning of period==

Example (Year ended 2020):
Spending (S) = $30M | Year-end VPF (2020, = beg 2021) = $500M | L.T. real geo avg return = 5%

$$ S(2021) = \frac{2}{3} \times $30\text{M} + \frac{1}{3} \times (5% \times $500\text{M}) = $20\text{M} + $8.333\text{M} = $28.333\text{M} $$

Why smooth? Pure % of current VPF transmits portfolio volatility directly into spending uncertainty. Smoothed policy stabilizes cash flows to the university.

Key Implications for Endowment Asset Allocation

  • ==Liquidity requirements are relatively low== (≈5.7% of VPF in example) → can ==allocate more to illiquid assets== (PE, HF, RE) to capture the illiquidity premium.
  • ==Perpetual horizon== (no maturity) supports higher allocation to illiquid/higher-risk assets.
  • Tolerating more short-term risk can ==produce higher long-term returns== → greater sustainability.

#EXAMPLE

  • Spending rate policy uses counter-cyclical smoothing: rates below 5% in strong return periods, above 5% in weak return periods
  • This design exacerbates endowment liquidity needs during severe market downturns
  • Smoothed policy stabilizes university cash flows, provides predictable distribution pattern, enables better resource deployment planning

F. Issues in Asset Manager Selection

Smaller endowments often hire external managers because in-house operations carry high fixed costs (staff, technology, compliance) and external managers provide access to diverse styles.

Internal focus shifts to: strategic asset allocation, overall risk management, and manager performance evaluation.

Ethical & Governance Considerations (Non-Arm’s Length Relationships)

  • Disclose any ==actual or perceived conflicts of interest== (e.g., external manager on university board or major donor).
  • Recuse conflicted parties from hiring/monitoring decisions.
  • Perform ongoing due diligence: understand strategy and verify performance against mandate. ==Avoid “set-it-and-forget-it”.==

H. Derivatives vs. Cash Market Rebalancing

Scenario: Strategic equity allocation = 70%, drifted to 62% after correction → need to increase equity exposure.

Cash Market Rebalancing (Trade Underlying)

  • Takes time (selection + execution) → performance drag.
  • Accumulates transaction costs + potential market impact (especially large trades).
  • Best for permanent or large rebalancing (buy-and-hold).

Derivatives Rebalancing (e.g., Long Equity Index Futures)

  • Highly liquid, low transaction costs, no market impact.
  • Instant exposure; easily reversed → ideal for temporary rebalancing.
  • Requires only margin (capital efficient).
  • Limitations: Tracking error if portfolio ≠ index; futures have expiries (roll risk, contango when r > y); generally unavailable for illiquid assets like PE (use cash market for those).

Rule of thumb: Use ==derivatives for temporary/tactical adjustments; cash market for structural changes or illiquid asset classes==.

#EXAMPLE

  • Use derivatives instead of cash for rebalancing when: - ==The sell-off is viewed as temporary and external manager performance is satisfactory==. - ==The goal is to rebalance without reallocating among external managers==.
    • High liquidity and flexibility are required to adjust/reverse exposures quickly.
  • Advantages of derivatives:
    • Quick implementation.
    • Tactical flexibility.
    • ==Ability to leave external managers in place==.
    • High liquidity.
  • Risks:
    • Derivatives introduce tracking error and operational risks, but these are mitigated by the short-term nature of the rebalancing.
  • Example:
    • ==Establish a $1.2%$ long position to the S&P 500 Index using short-term S&P 500 futures to rebalance US public equities==.

I. Tactical Asset Allocation (TAA)

TAA = short-term deviations from long-term Strategic Asset Allocation (SAA) to exploit perceived opportunities.

Example:
SAA: Stocks 60% ±5% [55–65%], Bonds 40% ±5% [35–45%].
Manager is bullish on stocks → TAA: Stocks 65%, Bonds 35% (+5% shift from bonds to stocks).

Implementation Tools (Asset-Class Views)

  • Long Equity Swap
  • Long Equity Futures
  • Long Equity Index ETFs

Capital Efficiency Comparison (Illustrative $5M shift)

  • Swaps & Futures: No (or minimal) upfront capital beyond margin; remainder earns R_f → high flexibility.
  • ETFs: Require full notional capital upfront → least capital efficient; potential cash drag.
  • Capital Commitment: Total return swap requires much lower cash outlay than unlevered ETF (full notional); derivatives are cash-efficient for incremental exposure
  • Liquidity: ETF more efficient — exchange-traded, standardized, narrow spreads; swap is OTC, negotiated, customizable (maturity, leverage, cost)
  • Tracking Error: Swap replicates index exactly (counterparty obligated to deliver index return); ETF has tracking error from NAV premiums/discounts, cash drag, regulatory diversification rules; swap introduces counterparty credit risk and net exposure management complexity

Key Implementation Notes

  • ETFs & futures = exchange-traded → more liquid. Swaps = OTC → less liquid + counterparty risk.
  • Futures: Daily mark-to-market (margin calls); must roll at expiry (contango risk if F > S_0 ==when carry cost r > yield y==).
  • Extracted text This implies that every time a futures is renewed, the Long has to lock in a Buy price that is higher than prevailing Spot Price.

Recall: $F = \frac{S_0(1+r)^t}{(1+y)^t}$

$r$: carry costs (i.e. interest rates) $y$: yield on the underlying $\therefore$ if $(r > y) \Rightarrow (F > S_0)$

  • Tracking error: Futures/swaps ≈ 0 to index; ETFs often have residual cash → higher TE.

ETFs• Most Cost-Effective: Lowest overall annual cost (~35 bps total: 15 bps price impact + 9.5 bps fee + tight bid-ask spreads).

• High Flexibility: Standardized and exchange-traded, making it easy to adjust/modify the exposure before the 1-year horizon.		• Least Cash-Efficient: Requires full cash outlay upfront (100% funding needed).

Futures• High Cash Efficiency: Requires significantly less upfront cash/margin.

Total Return Swaps• Zero Tracking Error: Exact index replication—the counterparty is contractually obligated to pay the exact total return of the S&P 500.
