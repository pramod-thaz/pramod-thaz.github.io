A. Approaches to Active Management

Goal: Beat benchmark. Two categories:

Fundamental (Discretionary)

  • Heavy manager judgment: company/industry analysis, DCF (absolute) or multiples (relative) valuation, financials, growth, leverage.
  • Portfolio: few stocks, large positions, industry/sector allocation, arbitrary limits (e.g., max 10% single stock), ==continuous monitoring.==
  • Risk: mostly idiosyncratic (company-specific). Success depends on manager insight.

Quantitative (Systematic / Rules-Based)

  • Model-driven (created upfront); manager expertise mainly at model construction.
  • Identify factors via statistical analysis of past data → avoid:
    • Survivorship bias (==include all stocks at start of period, not just current survivors==).
    • Look-ahead bias (==use only data available in real time;== e.g., use expected earnings, not actual released later).
  • Portfolio: baskets for factor exposures (e.g., long small-cap index + short large-cap for size tilt), factor allocation, optimization for weights (no arbitrary limits), ==periodic rules-based rebalancing.==
  • Risk: factor level (premiums may not persist). Success depends on model quality & predictive power (IC). #EXAMPLE portfolio/factor level risk monitoring

Key Differences:

  • Diversification: Fundamental → ==lower (higher idiosyncratic risk)==; Quant → higher (==factor risk==).
  • Allocation: Industry weights vs factor betas/exposures.
  • Monitoring: Continuous limit checks vs scheduled rebalance.
  • Quant & Fundamental have low correlation → excellent diversifiers when combined.
  • Quantitative uses historical data for decisions; fundamental (not quant) emphasizes forecasting future prospects/earnings/cash flows. #EXAMPLE

#EXAMPLE #contrast contruction process

Fundamental: Construct the ==portfolio by overweighting stocks that are expected to outperform their peers or the market as a whole.== Where necessary for risk reduction, underweight some benchmark stocks that are expected to underperform. ==The stocks that fell out in the pre-screening process do not have explicit forecasts and will not be included in the portfolio.==

Quantitative: Construct the portfolio by ==maximizing the objective function (such as portfolio alpha or information ratio) with risk models.==
#EXAMPLE rebalance process Fundamental: The manager ==monitors each stock continuously and sells stocks when their market prices surpass the target prices== (either through appreciation of the stock price or through reduction of the target price due to changes in expectations).

Quantitative: Portfolios are usually rebalanced at regular intervals, such as monthly.

B. BOTTOM UP (BU) ACTIVE STRATEGIES

Start at company level → identify strong candidates → value via DCF or multiples → buy if undervalued and price expected to converge to intrinsic value within investment horizon.

Company Qualities Sought:

  • Strong brand & sustainable business model (pricing power, margins).
  • Competitive advantage (tech, scarce resources, high barriers, distribution).
  • Superior management (long-term focus, high ROIC, low turnover, strong ESG, shareholder-aligned).

Value Strategies (cheap vs earnings/book): #EXAMPLE

  • Relative Value: Low P/E vs peers (but check fundamentals justify it; ==P/E not comparable across industries)==. #EXAMPLE ==compare to sector peers==
  • Contrarian: Buy when sentiment ==deeply bearish (overreaction);== expect rebound on earnings recovery.
  • ==High Quality Value: Cheap + financially strong, consistent earnings, high ROE.== #EXAMPLE High-quality value = attractive valuation metrics supported by financial strength/profitability (stocks can trade far from intrinsic value due to irrationality). High P/B vs. peers but still below intrinsic fits.
  • Income: High or growing dividend yield (defensive in downturns). #EXAMPLE
  • ==Deep Value: Distressed== companies trading at steep discount (irrational market pricing). #EXAMPLE
  • Restructure & Distress: Buy cheap during hardship; influence restructuring or profit from liquidation (acquire large blocks for influence).
  • ==Special Situations: Corporate events (M&A, spinoffs) — needs industry + legal expertise.==

C. TOP DOWN (TD) ACTIVE STRATEGIES

Start with macroeconomic forecast (economic cycle phase) → identify outperforming regions/sectors/styles → gain exposure via ETFs, futures, or BU stock selection within them.

Common Tactics: #imp

  • ==Sector Rotation:== Tilt to sectors expected to outperform in next cycle phase (use industry ETFs for broad exposure).
  • Style Rotation: Time small-cap vs large-cap, value vs growth, etc.
  • Volatility: If expected actual vol > implied vol → buy options (or long straddle if direction uncertain).
  • Thematic: Demographic/trend plays (e.g., aging population → healthcare stocks).
  • Overlays: Hedge unintended macro exposures (e.g., short currency futures on foreign holdings) or add active views (long another currency even without underlying exposure).

==Top-down: focuses on macro environment, demographic trends, & govt policies for investment decisions.== ==Top-down used in: country/geographic allocation, sector/industry rotation, equity style rotation, volatility-based strategies, & thematic investing.== #EXAMPLE

  • select countries to overweight or underweight
  • Once the country or region weights are determined by a top-down process, the portfolio can be constructed by selecting stocks in the relevant countries or regions.
  • A portfolio manager with expertise in identifying drivers of sector or industry returns will establish a view on those drivers and will set weights for those sectors in a portfolio. For example, the performance of the energy sector is typically driven by the price of crude oil. The returns of the materials sector rest on forecasts for commodity prices. Once a view is established on the return and risk of each sector, a manager can then decide which industries to invest in and what weightings to assign to those industries relative to the benchmark.
  • passive factor investing—sometimes marketed as “smart beta” products—has given portfolio managers more tools and flexibility for investing in different equity styles.
  • One can exploit the fact, for example, that high-quality stocks tend to perform well in recessions, or that cyclical deep-value companies are more likely to deliver superior returns in a more “risk-on” environment
  • where the ==investment mandate permits,== top-down managers can choose among different equity style ETFs and structured products to obtain risk exposures that are consistent with their views on different stages of the economic cycle or their views on market sentiment.

D. FACTOR-BASED ACTIVE STRATEGIES

Beyond CAPM (market only): rewarded systematic factors with long-term premiums.

Key Factors:

  • Size: Small-cap premium (higher risk).
  • Style (Value): Low P/E & P/B outperform growth (distress risk or behavioral overreaction).
  • Momentum: Prior winners continue (behavioral overreaction; can be industry-wide → sector risk). #EXAMPLE
  • price momentum has performed substantially better in Europe than in the United States. Managers should be aware that what appears to be impressive performance in backtests does not necessarily imply that the factor will continue to add value in the future.
  • Using the ==simple price momentum factor means that a portfolio buys past winners and shorts past losers.== The resulting portfolio could have ==exposure to potentially significant industry bets - By equalizing exposure across sectors, the strategy removes the risk of inadvertently making massive macroeconomic bets.== ==Sector-neutral price momentum== focuses on stock selection without such risk exposures and thus tends to reduce downside risk.
  • Quality: Consistent earnings + low debt (outperform in downturns); or high earnings quality (reported earnings close to CFO).

#EXAMPLE 2-month price increase is momentum—not growth (no earnings-growth proxy).

Construction Issues:

  • Non-standardized categories (e.g., small-cap cutoff? P/E threshold for value?).
  • Datamining risk → require economic rationale + out-of-sample testing.
  • Avoid ==survivorship/look-ahead bias in backtests.==
  • Premiums not guaranteed to persist.

Extracting Premiums (Long-Short): e.g., ==long smallest 10% + short largest 10% for size premium.== Drawbacks: Ignores middle 80%, assumes ==linearity== (risk may rise exponentially), ==concentrated portfolios, shorting restrictions, not pure factor (other exposures creep in)==.

Active Element: Factor tilting/rotation — adjust betas based on views (e.g., ↑ size beta if small-caps expected to outperform).

G. FUNDAMENTAL ACTIVE INVESTMENT STRATEGIES

7-Step Process: #imp #EXAMPLE

  1. Define investment universe (per mandate).
  2. Prescreen (e.g., P/E filter) → shortlist.
  3. In-depth company + industry analysis (==neutral weight if no view==).
  4. Project earnings & cash flows.
  5. Estimate intrinsic value (DCF/multiples); long undervalued, short overvalued; update continuously.
  6. Weight positions for optimal risk-adjusted return (client risk tolerance); ==add stop-losses.==
  7. Periodic rebalance to align with objectives. #EXAMPLE continuous monitoring of holdings; rebalance any time based on company-specific info.

Main Pitfalls:

  • Behavioral Biases (==cognitive & emotional==): #imp
    • Confirmation (seek confirming info only) → mitigate: broad information sources.
    • Illusion of Control → mitigate: strict position limits (IPS).
    • Availability (overweight recent/familiar) → mitigate: long-term focus.
    • Loss Aversion (hold losers too long) → mitigate: automatic stop-losses.
    • Overconfidence (overestimate skill) → mitigate: thorough performance attribution.
    • Regret Aversion (avoid action or hold too long) → mitigate: periodic position review + rationale.
  • Value Trap: Appears cheap but fundamentals deteriorating (==need turnaround catalyst==). #EXAMPLE
  • Growth Trap: ==Pay high price for growth that fails to materialize (competition or maturation) → sharp price drop.==

#EXAMPLE The portfolio manager has a ==growth orientation with a focus on companies that have delivered EPS growth in recent years and that have maintained their earnings and price growth momentum.== Criterion 1 specifies accelerating EPS growth rates over recent fiscal years, while criterion 2 discards companies for which recent earnings growth has been negative. I. EQUITY INVESTMENT STYLES

Two Dimensions:

  1. Stock Characteristics: Relative valuation (Value/Growth/Blend), Market cap (small/mid/large), Price momentum, Volatility, Dividend yield, Earnings quality.
  2. Membership Based: Sector/Industry, Country of origin, Market type (Developed/Developing/Frontier).

Good Categorization Conditions: ==High correlation within group, low correlation between groups.==

Benefits: Clear style from mandate, proper benchmarking (avoid false “active” signal), fair peer comparison.

==Style== Identification Methods: #imp

  • Holding-Based (HB): Classify each holding bottom-up → aggregate. Most accurate/granular (if data available). Issue: ==ambiguous breakpoints== (e.g., small-cap cutoff $3B vs $10B changes classification); value/growth overlap possible.
  • Return-Based (RB): Regress portfolio returns vs style indexes (useful when holdings undisclosed). Captures historical tilts (good for rotation managers). Issues: ==limited/ imperfect style indexes; may treat non-index exposures as idiosyncratic; broad indexes may miss specific strategies (e.g., GARP vs broad growth).==

HB vs RB: HB more precise when holdings known; RB better for history/rotation and non-disclosed portfolios. Use together (RB broad screen + HB deep dive). Categories can still be too broad (performance varies within same style due to other factor exposures).

THE MORNINGSTAR STYLE BOX In a style box, each style pair splits the stock universe into two to three groups, such as value, core (or “blend”), and growth. The same universe can be split by another style definition—for example, large cap, mid cap, and small cap. It uses holdings-based style analysis and classifies about the same number of stocks in each of the value, core, and growth styles. Two of the three style categories are “value” and “growth,” common to both stocks and funds. However, the third, central column is ==labeled “core” for stocks== (i.e., those stocks for which neither value nor growth characteristics dominate) ==and “blend” for funds== (meaning that the fund holds a mixture of growth and value stocks).

For each stock, Morningstar assigns a growth score and a value score, each based on five components. The scores are scaled to a range of 0 to 100, and the difference between the stock’s growth and value scores is called the net style score. If this net style score is strongly negative, approaching -100, the stock’s style is classified as value. If the result is strongly positive, the stock is classified as growth. If the scores for value and growth are similar in strength, the net style score will be close to zero and the stock will be classified as core.

MSCI INDEXES The value investment style characteristics for index construction are defined using three variables: ==book-value-to-price ratio, 12-month forward-earnings-to-price ratio, and dividend yield.== The growth investment style characteristics for index construction are defined using five variables: ==long-term forward EPS growth rate, short-term forward EPS growth rate, current internal growth rate, long-term historical EPS growth trend, and long-term historical sales-per-share growth trend==. Z-scores for each variable are calculated and aggregated for each security to determine the security’s overall style characteristics. For example, a stock is assigned a so-called “value inclusion factor” of 0.6, which means that the stock could have both value and growth characteristics and contributes to the performance of the value and growth indexes by 60% and 40%, respectively
