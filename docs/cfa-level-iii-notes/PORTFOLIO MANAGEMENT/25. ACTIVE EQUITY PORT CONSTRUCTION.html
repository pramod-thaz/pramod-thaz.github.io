C. Alpha Skills

Two Sources:

  1. ==Security Selection== (β held constant): Overweight outperformers, underweight underperformers.
    • ==Sector/Region selection also α (no evidence of systematic premium unlike size).==
  2. ==Factor Timing== (tactical β deviations): ↑β before factor premium rises; ↓β before it falls.
    • Policy/systematic β (replicable via indexes) = passive (not α).
    • Tactical deviations only = true α.
    • ==Net client benefit only if management fee < α.==

E. Constructing the Portfolio – Breadth (BR)

Fundamental Law of Active Management: $E(AR) = IC \times TC \times \sqrt{BR} \times TE$

  • IC (Information Coefficient): Forecast accuracy (==corr predicted vs actual return==). ↑IC → ↑AR.
  • TC (Transfer Coefficient): Implementation skill (ideas → actual positions). TC=1 (no constraints), TC=0 (passive/index).
  • BR (Breadth): # independent decisions/opportunities. ==Low BR (e.g., 50 oil stocks = bet on oil price) → high idiosyncratic risk, unreliable AR.==
  • TE: Target active risk (client tolerance).

==To achieve +AR long-term: IC > 0, TC > 0, TE > 0, BR > 0 (must have opportunities - long or short).==

F. Approaches to Active Portfolio Construction

Systematic (Quant/Rules-Based) vs Discretionary (Fundamental):

  • Systematic: Pre-set rules/model; factor exposures (rewarded premiums); ==diversified== (min idiosyncratic); optimization (max IR); ==manager discretion mainly at model design.==
  • Discretionary: ==Manager judgment per trade==; ==α via mispricing or timing;== ==concentrated==; less structured.

Top-Down (TD) vs Bottom-Up (BU):

  • TD: Macro → sectors/industries → best stocks within; ==α from factor timing.==
  • BU: Company level (mispriced securities regardless of sector); α from security selection. More common in practice (e.g., deep-value).
  • Hybrids common; ==both can produce concentrated or diversified portfolios.==

G. Active Share vs Active Risk (TE)

Active Share = $½ Σ |W_p,i - W_b,i|]

Active Risk (TE) = volatility (sd) of AR; incorporates ==correlations== (unlike Active Share).

==Key Relationships (they do not always move together):== #imp

  • Neutral factor exposures + high Active Share → most TE from security selection.
  • Many stocks in PF/bench → smaller weight differences → lower Active Share impact on TE.

H. Risk Budgeting

Allocate total tolerated risk across dimensions (==factors, securities, sectors, countries, etc.==).

4 Steps:

  1. Identify measures: Absolute (σ) for benchmark-agnostic; Relative (TE) for benchmark-aware. For example, a long/short equity manager benchmarked against a cash plus target will usually prefer an absolute risk measure (such as total vol￾atility of portfolio returns), whereas a long-only equity manager bench￾marked against a capitalization-weighted index may prefer a relative risk measure (such as active risk).
  2. ==Map how decisions== (β tilts, security selection, factor timing) contribute to total risk. Total portfolio variance may be dominated by exposure to rewarded risk factors or by allocations to countries, sectors, or securities. ==If these exposures are dynamic, the timing of portfolio exposures also introduces risk. An important step in risk budgeting is to understand what drives a portfolio’s risk and to ensure the portfolio has the right kinds of specific risks.==
  3. Set target ==budget== (subjective; higher for active/high-conviction managers; lower for risk-averse). ● Targeted levels of risk vary widely among managers and strategies. Although there are general principles that limit the level of advisable risk in a specific strategy, it is also very much a policy issue.
    • Implementation Constraints & IR: Without limits, ↑ TE allows proportional ↑ Active Return via leverage + shorting → IR stays constant. ==With constraints (position limits, no leverage/short, illiquidity) → Active Return rises slower than TE (due to higher trading costs or weaker ideas) → IR falls.== #EXAMPLE
    • High Risk Targets & Diversification: Most stocks have ~market risk. ==At very high risk budgets, eligible securities become scarce → portfolio concentrates → ↑ idiosyncratic risk== → lower Sharpe Ratio (sub-optimal risk-adjusted returns). Market-risk portfolio: easy to diversify away idio risk. ==on the other hand, 2× market-risk portfolio: limited names → concentrated.==
    • Leverage & Geometric vs Arithmetic Return: ↑ Leverage → ↑ volatility → geometric (compounded) return declines relative to arithmetic mean (volatility drag). ==Higher turnover/volatility erodes the realized compounded growth rate even if arithmetic expected return looks attractive.==
  4. Allocate efficiently considering ==position sizing & correlations (more corr = less diversifications, so less bets).==

Practical Limits on High Risk Budgets: #imp see explanation above

  • Constraints (no leverage/shorts, illiquidity) → diminishing IR.
  • High volatility → lower geometric (compounded) returns vs arithmetic.
  • Fewer eligible securities at very high risk → less diversification → sub-optimal Sharpe/IR.
  • Specialize risk budget where manager has edge; hedge non-specialist risks (e.g., currency for stock-picker).

K. Risk Measures & Constraints for Equity Portfolios

Determining the Appropriate Level of Risk (Curriculum pp. 163–165)

Overview

Establishing the right level of absolute risk ((\sigma)) or active risk (TE / tracking error) is subjective and depends on:

  • ==Investment style==
  • ==Conviction in ability to add value==
  • ==Available implementation tools (long-only vs. long-short, leverage, liquidity, position limits)==

Example Risk Targets by Mandate

  • Market-neutral hedge fund: ==Absolute risk ≈ 10%==
  • Closet indexer (long-only): Active risk < 2%
  • Benchmark-agnostic long-only: ==Active risk 6–10%==
  • Benchmark-agnostic: Absolute risk ==≈ 85% of index risk==

==Three practical limits== that can degrade risk-adjusted performance if risk is pushed too high:

  1. Implementation constraints (degrade Information Ratio)
  2. Limited diversification opportunities (degrade Sharpe Ratio)
  3. Excessive leverage (reduces compounded/geometric return)

Purpose: Limit losses from unexpected events.

1. Heuristic Constraints (rules of thumb; Bottom-Up outcome):

  • Position limits (single stock, sector, region; e.g., max 3% or 5× bench weight).
  • Factor exposures, currency, leverage, illiquid stocks (e.g., ≤20% ADV), turnover.
  • Risk is outcome, not explicit input. #EXAMPLE

Because ==no single position or bet has dominated historical returns, a heuristic constraint on maximum position size is a logical one==. Given that his portfolio is built around a relatively small number of positions (80), single positions can easily remain in the portfolio for two or three years. #EXAMPLE ![](_rect_imgs/25. X ACTIVE EQUITY PORT CONSTRUCTION - merged__PMAX_Q_A_25_ACTIVE_EQUITY_INVESTING_PORTFOLIO_CONSTRUCTION_p5_68-570.png) 2. Formal / Statistical Constraints (historical distributions):

  • Standard Deviation (σ)
  • Active Risk / TE
  • Skewness (positive/negative tails) #EXAMPLE Skewness = asymmetry of return distribution. Constraint “no more than 60% of deviations from mean are negative” directly limits negative skew.
  • Drawdown (peak-to-trough loss)
  • ==Value at Risk (VaR): e.g., 5% daily VaR $2M = 5% chance of ≥$2M loss in a day (or 1 in 20 days)==
    • Measures the ==minimum loss t==hat may be experienced. For example, a 5% daily VaR of $2M, would imply that there is a ==5% probability that the portfolio can experience a $2M loss or more in any given day==. Alternatively, we can say that out of every 20 days, there will be one day (i.e. 1/20 = 5%) in which we can expect the loss to exceed $2M.
  • vi) Conditional VaR (CVaR): While VaR simply indicates the minimum loss that is possible, CVaR estimates what the average loss would be if the VaR threshold was breached. For example, while the VaR may be $2M, CVaR might be $2.7M (i.e. whenever the portfolio loss exceeded $2M, it averaged to around a $2.7M loss).
  • vii) Incremental VaR (IVaR): Measures the change in portfolio VaR as a result of adding a new stock (and thus lowering the weight of an existing stock).
  • viii) Marginal VaR (MVaR): Measures the effect that a very small change in the weight of the existing securities would have on portfolio VaR

L. Considerations in Portfolio Construction (AUM, Costs, Slippage, Constraints)

AUM Trade-offs (Explicit vs Implicit Costs)

  • Low AUM: ↑ explicit costs (higher % commissions, no volume discounts) but ↓ implicit costs (market impact negligible; trades don’t move price).
  • High AUM: ↓ explicit costs (negotiated commissions) but ↑ implicit costs (esp. small caps; large trades cause slippage/impact).
  • Net: High-AUM mgrs often constrained; low-AUM mgrs more agile but pay more in % terms.

Market Impact (Implicit Cost) ↑ When

  • Trade size large vs ADV (avg daily volume)
  • Stock illiquid
  • Portfolio turnover high
  • Execution urgent/immediate
  • Trade sends info signal (e.g., large institutional sell → price drops)

Mitigation for Info Signal: Break into smaller orders or use “unlit” venues (crossing networks). Long-term/passive investors can wait for liquidity.

High-AUM / Small-Cap Constraints (to control impact)

  • Position limit = f(mkt cap) e.g. index weight ±2%
  • Position limit = f(ADV) e.g. max 25% of ADV
  • Longer lags between rebalances (↓ turnover)
    → Risk: Becomes concentrated in largest/most liquid names or forced to diversify across more holdings.

Slippage Cost
= Decision price (mid of bid-ask at decision time) – Execution price
Incorporates: Stock vol, price trend, market impact itself.
Higher for small caps (often dominates explicit commissions).
Small-cap fund mitigations: Limit AUM (close to new investors), ↑ # holdings, ↓ turnover (buy-and-hold), break large orders, concentrate on liquid small/mid names, tilt toward mid/large caps.

Portfolio mgmt costs: explicit & implicit (delay & slippage)

  • Slippage costs ↑ for smaller-cap securities & during high volatility
  • Immediate execution → ↑ market impact costs #EXAMPLE
  • Fund 1 (large AUM + small-cap focus + small # securities + large trades) faces highest market-impact costs. Small-cap liquidity + large trade size relative to ADV = big implicit costs.
  • Patient mgr can mitigate market impact by slowly building positions as liquidity allows, but faces ↑ volatility/trend price risk

  3. ==Effective risk management (volatility controlled)==

N. Long-Only (L/O) Strategies

Merits

  1. Risk Premiums: ==Capture size/value/etc. premia over LT== (ST volatile/negative possible)
  2. Capacity & Scalability: ==Easier to buy large sizes than short==
  3. Limited Liability: Max loss = capital invested (price → 0)
  4. Regulations: Some jurisdictions ban shorting → L/O only option
  5. Transaction Simplicity: No borrow, collateral, lender calls, or lending fees
  6. Lower Mgmt Fees (vs L/S)
  7. Ideology: Provides capital to firms; shorting profits from misfortune; prices trend up LT → shorting unsustainable for many

O. Long-Short (L/S) Strategies

Merits

  1. Earn α both sides (long underpriced + short overpriced); most unconstrained strategy
    • Highest Transfer Coefficient (TC) → highest expected Active Return (AR) & Information Ratio (IR)
    • Short proceeds can ↑ long weights or add more α ideas
  2. Hedge unwanted risks while keeping α (e.g., long undervalued pharma stocks + short pharma index to neutralize industry)
    • Neutralizing industry/sector → lower TE (industry deviations hurt TE more than single-stock deviations)
  3. Full extraction of factor premium (e.g., long small-caps + short large-caps → pure size premium; market risk cancels)
    • ==Efficient use of leverage (short lower-return factor, fund higher-return factor)==
    • Diversification + controlled factor exposures

Risks

  1. Unlimited loss on shorts (price can rise without limit)
  2. Higher leverage → ==higher TE vs long-only benchmark==
  3. Operational complexity & costs: Borrow fees, collateral (100% for high-quality; more for low-quality), lender recall risk
  4. Short squeeze: Rising price forces collective covering → price spikes further
  5. Neutralizing a factor also eliminates its premium

Key Metrics

  • Net Exposure = |Long| – |Short|
    (+ = net long; – = net short; 0 = market neutral)
  • Gross Exposure = |Long| + |Short| (higher = more aggressive/leverage)

A compromise between:

  • ==↓ risk vs. not fully capturing market risk premium==
  • ↑ return potential from alpha & other risk premiums at ==potential expense of ↑ active risk==
  • ==Greater diversification vs. ↑ costs & complexity==

#EXAMPLE

A long-only factor investor is limited in his ability to short (relative to the benchmark) positions that do not have the desired characteristics. Adding the ability to lever the portfolio and to take short positions ==allows the manager to more fully express his views on both the winners and the losers.== ==This increased ability to translate insights into positions (higher transfer coefficient) leads to a higher information ratio.== In addition, the long/short product ==neutralizes the Market factor, allowing the manager to focus on the other rewarded factors that are the source of his skill.==

Exam Tips:

  • Distinguish rewarded factor exposure (systematic, not α) from true skill (α).
  • Factor timing α only from tactical deviations, not policy β.
  • ==Active Share ≠ Active Risk (correlations matter for latter).==
  • Breadth (BR) is critical — concentrated bets on one theme = low BR, high idiosyncratic risk.
  • ==Risk budgeting: allocate where you have edge; constraints reduce IR at high TE levels.==
  • Long horizon needed to separate α from ε.

P. Long-Extension (L/E) Strategies (“Enhanced Active”)

Definition: ==Constrained L/S== (e.g., 130/30)

  • Start with $100 capital → Long $130, Short $30
  • Net Exposure = 100; Gross Exposure = 160

Benefits vs Pure L/O

  • Exploit more α opportunities (use short proceeds to extend longs)
  • Increase exposure to attractive factors (e.g., raise small-cap β above 100%)
  • Provides some leverage while remaining benchmark-aware

Risks: Same as L/S but lesser degree (constrained leverage/shorting).

Q. Market-Neutral (M/N) Strategies

Definition: L/S ==with β(mkt) = 0== (or any chosen factor β = 0).
Purpose: Avoid needing market-timing skill; focus purely on other factors (size, value, etc.); portable α.

Construction Example
Long $100 undervalued stocks (β_mkt = 1.2)
Short enough overvalued stocks (β_mkt = 1.5) to net zero:
W_L × β_L – W_S × β_S = 0
==(e.g., short ~$80 to offset).==

Can neutralize any systematic factor → create pure factor portfolios (uncorrelated factors → excellent diversification when combined).

Self-Financing & Portability

  • Shorts can fully fund longs → little/no own capital needed for α portion.
  • Use own capital to equitize (buy index) → separate α (M/N) from β (index).
  • Portable: Add M/N sleeve to any existing portfolio.
  • Diversification benefit: α uncorrelated with β.

Lower overall risk than L/O (unwanted risks diversified away) but eliminates that risk’s premium too.

Limitations
==i. βs unstable over time → hard to maintain neutrality (requires dynamic rebalancing).==
==ii. Zero β → no participation in market upside → must equitize with index to capture β return.==
