CDS Pricing

  • Price: The quoted clean price of the CDS (where a price of 1.00 means it is trading at par).
  • Fixed Coupon ($c$): The standardized, fixed premium rate of the contract (e.g., 1% or 5%).
  • CDS Spread ($s$): The current market spread for a new contract of the same maturity.
  • Duration: The risky presentation value of a basis point (RPV01), or effective spread duration. [2, 3, 6, 7, 8]

The Derivation and Market Intuition

The formula is derived from the Upfront Premium calculation. [6, 9]

Step 1: Calculate the Upfront Premium

When you buy protection, the market thinks you should be paying the Current Market Spread ($s$). If the contract’s Fixed Coupon ($c$) is different, you must settle the difference upfront:
$$\text{Upfront Premium Paid by Buyer} \approx (\text{CDS Spread} - \text{Fixed Coupon}) \times \text{Duration}$$

  • If the market spread is higher than the fixed coupon, the buyer pays an upfront premium to the seller.
  • If the market spread is lower, the buyer receives an upfront premium from the seller. [3, 4]

Step 2: Convert to the Quoted Bond-Like Price

To make credit derivatives look and trade like standard corporate bonds, the industry developed a standardized pricing convention. The quoted price is defined as:
$$\text{Price} = 1 - \text{Upfront Premium Paid by Buyer}$$

Substitute the Upfront Premium formula into this convention:
$$\text{Price} = 1 - [(\text{CDS Spread} - \text{Fixed Coupon}) \times \text{Duration}]$$

Distribute the negative sign inside the brackets:
$$\mathbf{\text{Price} = 1 + [(\text{Fixed Coupon} - \text{CDS Spread}) \times \text{Duration}]}$$ [!!sticky-note|REVIEW|var(--color-red-rgb)]

Important Catch: The Inverse Relationship

Because this formula mirrors bond pricing conventions, a higher price does not mean protection is more valuable to buy; it means credit risk has decreased. [1]

  • If Credit Risk Falls (Spreads Narrow): The CDS Spread drops. The math creates a positive number inside the brackets, causing the Price to rise above 1 (e.g., 1.03 or 103% of par). [1, 6]
  • If Credit Risk Rises (Spreads Widen): The CDS Spread spikes. The subtraction yields a negative number, pushing the Price below 1 (e.g., 0.95 or 95% of par). [1, 5, 10]

Therefore, a protection buyer is effectively shorting this price index—they profit when this quoted price goes down. [1, 5]

R. CDS Strategies: Changing Credit Exposure (Bottom-Up)

Example: Investor wants to reduce portfolio exposure to Bond L.
CDS on Bond L: Term = 3 years, Notional = $100m, Duration = 3.74 years, Prevailing CDS spread = 1.4%.

Step 1: Undercapitalizing an exposure implicitly assumes the investor expects risks (and spreads) to increase.
→ Buy Protection (short credit via CDS). [!!sticky-note|REVIEW|var(--color-red-rgb)]

Step 2: If spreads rise to 1.6%:

$$ % \Delta V_{\text{CDS}} = (D) \times (\Delta \text{sp}) $$

where (\Delta \text{sp} = ==(1.6% - 1.4%) = 0.2%==)

$$ % \Delta V_{\text{CDS}} = (3.74) \times (0.2%) = 0.748% $$

$$ \Delta V_{\text{CDS}} = 0.748% \times $100\text{m} = $748{,}000 \text{ gain} $$

W. Global Credit Strategies – Developed vs. Emerging Markets

9 Key Differences (Exam Favorite – Memorize Structure):

  1. Issuers & Derivatives Liquidity: Developed = wide variety + liquid CDS/derivatives market → easier complex strategies. ==Emerging = many state-owned/sponsored issuers → fewer actively traded bonds.==
  2. Industry Diversification: Developed = broad sectors. ==Emerging = concentrated in few industries → higher volatility.==
  3. Instrument Variety: Developed = bonds + active MBS/ABS/CDOs etc. E==merging = dominated by bonds.==
  4. Accounting Standards: Significant differences → harder cross-border peer comparisons.
  5. Exchange Rate Regime & Monetary Policy: ==Fixed peg → stability but loss of independent monetary policy (must mirror anchor currency central bank) → more crisis-prone.==
  6. Currency Volatility (Floating Regimes): Emerging currencies far more volatile. Yield advantage easily erased (e.g., 3% extra yield wiped out by >3% currency depreciation).
  7. Political Risk: Higher in emerging markets. Assess sovereign ability + willingness to repay, rule of law, transparency, capital controls risk.
  8. Sovereign Rating Ceiling: Sovereign rating caps all issuers in jurisdiction (most emerging sovereigns at lower end of IG).
  9. Credit Quality Drivers & Ratios (GDP-focused):
    • External Debt / GDP (foreign-currency debt burden; cannot print FX).
    • Reserves / GDP (buffer for foreign obligations). #EXAMPLE

==The government budget deficit as a percentage of GDP is a gauge of fiscal stability for the domestic economy, while the external debt-to-GDP ratio is a measure of financial leverage to foreign lenders.== ==Currency reserves as a percentage of GDP measure the available liquidity in foreign currency to meet external obligations.==

Exam Note: Emerging market excess yield is compensation for these amplified risks (currency, political, concentration, liquidity). Always evaluate sovereign ceiling and willingness-to-pay separately from ability-to-pay.

Credit Strategies across Developed Markets

1. Overall Investment Thesis and Positioning

The manager expects a stronger economic recovery in Europe than in the United States. Specifically:

  • European high-yield credit spreads are expected to narrow by 25%.
  • US credit spreads and expected losses are expected to decline by only 10%.
  • The euro is expected to appreciate by 1% against the US dollar.

To capitalize on the greater spread tightening in Europe, the manager should take long credit exposure to European high-yield debt.

Since the vehicle is a CDS index, this is achieved by selling protection on the iTraxx-Xover (the 5-year European high-yield CDS index). Selling protection means the manager receives the fixed 5% premium and will profit if European spreads tighten as expected.

2. US Corporate Bond Portfolio – Expected Excess Return

Under the manager’s scenario, all US OAS levels and expected loss rates decline by 10%.

3. iTraxx-Xover Valuation – Detailed Explanation (Including “95.75 per $104.25”)

This is the most technical part of the solution.

Base CDS Index Valuation Formula

[!!sticky-note|REVIEW|var(--color-red-rgb)] When a CDS index uses a standardized fixed coupon (here 5%), the position has an upfront value. The approximate value to the protection seller is:

$$ \text{Value to Protection Seller} \approx (\text{Fixed Coupon} - \text{Market Spread}) \times \text{EffSpreadDur}_{\text{CDS}} $$

Initial situation:

  • Fixed Coupon = 5%
  • Market Spread = 400 bps = 4%
  • EffSpreadDur_CDS = 4.25

$$ \text{Initial Value} = (5% - 4%) \times 4.25 = 1% \times 4.25 = +4.25% \text{ of notional} $$

Where “95.75 per $104.25” Comes From

The original solution expresses this same 4.25% upfront value in two equivalent ways:

  • From the protection buyer’s perspective: They must pay 4.25 points upfront → quoted “price” = 95.75 (i.e., 100 – 4.25).
  • From the protection seller’s perspective: They receive 4.25 points upfront → their position is worth 104.25 (i.e., 100 + 4.25).

This “95.75 per $104.25” phrasing is simply showing both sides of the same transaction using an older bond-price quotation style. It is not required for calculations.

The solution immediately gives the cleaner and preferred expression — the Price Factor:

$$ \text{Price Factor} = 1 + \bigl[ \text{EffSpreadDur}_{\text{CDS}} \times (\text{Fixed Coupon} - \text{Market Spread}) \bigr] $$

$$ \text{Initial Price Factor} = 1 + (4.25 \times 1%) = \mathbf{1.0425} $$

This 1.0425 is the standard way to represent the value of the protection-selling position.

4. New Price Factor After European Spread Tightening

European high-yield spreads narrow by 25% → iTraxx-Xover market spread falls to 300 bps (3%).

$$ \text{New Value to Protection Seller} = (5% - 3%) \times 4.25 = 2% \times 4.25 = +8.50% \text{ of notional} $$

$$ \text{New Price Factor} = 1 + (4.25 \times 2%) = \mathbf{1.085} $$

5. Return Calculations

[!!sticky-note|REVIEW|var(--color-red-rgb)] Return in euro terms:

$$ R_{\text{Euro}} = \frac{1.085 - 1.0425}{1.0425} = \frac{0.0425}{1.0425} \approx \mathbf{4.077%} $$

Convert to US dollar return (using the currency return formula):

$$ R_{\text{USD}} = (1 + R_{\text{Euro}}) \times (1 + R_{\text{FX}}) - 1 $$

Where ( R_{\text{FX}} = +1% ) (euro appreciation).

$$ R_{\text{USD}} = (1 + 4.077%) \times (1 + 1%) - 1 = 1.04077 \times 1.01 - 1 = \mathbf{5.118%} $$

6. Overall Portfolio Expected Return

The iTraxx-Xover position is sized to match the US high-yield bond allocation. This effectively gives the iTraxx-Xover a 50% weight in the total strategy.

$$ \text{Total Expected Return} = 3.30% + \frac{5.118%}{2} = 3.30% + 2.559% = \mathbf{5.86%} $$

Summary of Key Insights

  • The manager sells protection on iTraxx-Xover to profit from expected stronger European spread tightening.
  • The “95.75 per $104.25” is simply an alternative way of expressing the initial +4.25% upfront value from both the buyer’s and seller’s perspectives. The clean version used for calculations is the Price Factor = 1.0425.
  • The Price Factor method ((1 + \text{Duration} \times (\text{Fixed Coupon} - \text{Market Spread}))) is the standard way to measure mark-to-market gains on CDS indices with standardized coupons.
  • The 4.25 duration amplifies the benefit of the 100 bps spread tightening.
  • Euro appreciation adds an extra 1% tailwind when converting returns back to USD.
  • The final 5.86% is the blended expected excess return of the combined US corporate bond portfolio + European CDS index strategy.

X. Structured Financial Instruments (ABS / Securitization)

Securitization Process (4 Steps):

  1. Bank/originator places loans in a pool.
  2. Pool/SPV issues ABS securities (tranches).
  3. Investors buy ABS shares.
  4. Proceeds go to originator (bank). Future loan cash flows (net of servicing fees) flow to ABS investors.

Benefits:

  • Converts illiquid loans into tradable securities.
  • Investors get pure exposure to underlying asset class (e.g., mortgages) without the originator’s other businesses.
  • Custom risk/return: aggressive investors pick high-risk/high-yield pools; conservative pick guaranteed ones.

Tranche Structure (Risk Redistribution) – Worked Example Pool = $60M loans.

  • Senior Tranche K: $25M par
  • Junior Tranche L: $35M par

Cash Flow Waterfall (Principal – Scheduled + Unscheduled/Prepayments):

  • All principal repayments go first to Senior K until K is fully paid off ($25M repaid).
  • Only then do remaining principal payments go to Junior L.

→ Senior K: Lower risk/return (capital protection).
→ Junior L: Higher risk/return (absorbs losses first; higher yield).

Economic Outlook Application:

  • Expect contraction (defaults ↑) → Buy Senior Tranche K (protection).
  • Expect expansion (defaults ↓) → Buy Junior Tranche L (higher yield).

Specific Instrument Types:

  • MBS: Backed by residential/commercial mortgages.
  • ==CDO: Backed by pool of private loans / corporate debt.==
  • ==CLO: Backed by floating-rate loans.== not targeted #EXAMPLE
  • Covered Bond: ==Senior debt issued by bank/financial institution. Dual recourse: (1) issuer’s general cash flows + (2) segregated cover pool (mortgages/loans). Stronger protection than plain ABS.== #EXAMPLE

As the housing sector slows and default rates rise, credit spreads of lower-rated MBS tend to widen by more than those of higher-rated MBS. The investor retains exposure to real estate cash flows while reducing exposure to spread widening. The shift to a BB rated credit card ABS tranche increases credit exposure, while the switch from BBB rated to A rated CDOs represents a reduction in overall market risk rather than a more targeted underweight, as in C.

Y. Analytical Tools for Fixed-Income Portfolios

Generic Process (Inputs → Model → Outputs)

I. Inputs

  1. Positions: Specific securities, amounts, long/short.
  2. Market Data: Live/real-time prices for continuous valuation.
  3. Credit & ESG ratings for each holding.
  4. Issuer financial information (from statements).

II. Model Parameters

  1. Time horizon (affects return/risk projections; 1yr vs. 10yr very different).
  2. Portfolio constraints (leverage limits, derivatives allowed?, shorting allowed? — affects exploitability of mispricings).
  3. VaR methodology (historical simulation vs. analytical/parametric).

III. Outputs

  1. Summary statistics (portfolio + benchmark): Return, spreads, duration, convexity, tracking error, top holdings, etc.
  2. Breakdowns: Sector weights, credit ratings, currency exposure, geographic allocation.
  3. Projected future cash flows (coupons + principal repayments) with timing.

Uses:

  • Assess overall risk/return positioning.
  • Simulate trade impact (e.g., shifting 10% to MBS → effect on portfolio duration, convexity, spread).
  • Check alignment with mandate (index ETF manager cares most about tracking error; active manager may focus on Sharpe ratio).
