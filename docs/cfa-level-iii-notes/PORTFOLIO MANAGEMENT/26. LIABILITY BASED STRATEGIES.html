This risk is minimized by selecting the portfolio with the lower convexity (and dispersion of cash flows)== Note that ==default risk is neglected in this discussion because the portfolio consists of government bonds that presumably have default probabilities approaching zero.==

C. MANAGING MULTIPLE LIABILITIES USING BONDS

#EXAMPLE

Company wants to use excess cash in order to retire its debt. Strategies available:

  1. Tender Offer: Buy back its bonds in the open market. However, this would almost certainly mean having to pay a premium in order to induce all bondholders to relinquish their bonds.
  2. Cash Flow Match (Defeasement): Use excess cash to purchase Government bonds whose cash inflow would exactly match (and therefore offset) the CFs due on the firm’s debt. ==This would allow the firm to remove the debt (and the matching portfolio) from its balance sheet.== However, since Government bond yields will be less than corporate yields, the firm would be required to purchase far more Government bonds than the balance outstanding on its debt. ==In effect then, this strategy is similar to paying a premium in order to retire the debt (just like a tender offer).==
  3. Duration Matching: Use the excess cash to construct a portfolio with the following parameters:
  4. i. ==Basis Point Value (BPV) of the portfolio matches that of the liability.== This will ensure that the portfolio value will change by the same amount as the value of the liabilities in ==response to a parallel change== in interest rates.
  5. ii. ==The portfolio’s convexity is slightly higher than that of the liability==. This will ensure that the portfolio will respond relatively better than the liabilities to non-parallel shifts in the yield curve. Part 1: ==Duration matching will require less capital (and therefore is the less expensive option). The manager has the flexibility to purchase any number of bonds, so long as the duration matching parameters are met. For example, the manager can match the BPV of the liability while still purchasing higher yielding bonds (thus reducing the amount of capital investment required)==. However, since the bond CFs would not be an exact match to the liability payments, the ==immunization will not be perfect.== As well, unlike the case of defeasement, ==the firm would still be required to report its debt on its balance sheet== (as the offset between the portfolio and the debt would not be perfect)

Part 2: Portfolio B best suits the duration matching strategy. It meets the 2 conditions:  ==It’s BPV of 64.51 closely matches the liability BPV of 64.47==. In other words, if there was a 1bp increase in interest rates, both the portfolio and liability would decrease by the same amount (approximately ¥64.5M)  ==Portfolio B’s convexity of 50.11 slightly exceeds that of the liability convexity (46.08).==

Strategies: ==CF Matching (exact CF match: no RRR/price risk) / Duration Matching / Contingent Imz.==

CF Matching: Select Bonds whose CFs exactly match timing and amount of liability CFs Duration Matching: ==BPV_PF = BPV_L== (BPV = D × V × 0.0001).

I. BOND INDEXES

Adv: Low fees, diversification, low underperf risk.
The primary advantages of ==running a portfolio to mimic an index are as== follows: i) Lower management fees (due to the absence of active analysis). ii) Diversification (as most indexes provide broad coverage). iii) Reduced risk of underperformance (as the aim is to remain at par with the index)

==Approaches: Pure (all bonds exact weights) / Enhanced (sample matching risk factors) / Active (deviate from index primary risk factors)==.

Issues: ==OTC illiquid, valuation (matrix), rebalance (maturing), bums problem (value-wtd large debtors).==

Pure Indexing impractical (tx/illiquidity) → better to use Enhanced (match D, KRD, sector/quality/spread D, embedded options, issuer).
